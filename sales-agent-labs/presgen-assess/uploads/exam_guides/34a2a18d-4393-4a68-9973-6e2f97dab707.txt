The Associate Certified Coach (ACC) Candidate Exam: Mastery Study Guide for Core Competencies, Ethics, and Professional Boundaries




A. Executive Summary and Examination Overview




A.1. Introduction to the ACC Credential and Exam Purpose


The International Coaching Federation (ICF) Associate Certified Coach (ACC) credential represents a significant global benchmark, signaling a professional commitment to the highest standards of ethical and proficient practice within the coaching field.1 The credential verifies that an individual possesses foundational coaching skills, knowledge, and competence.1 ACC holders are required to demonstrate a thorough understanding of the ICF Core Competencies, the ICF Code of Ethics, and the established definition of coaching.1
To be eligible for the ACC credential, applicants must satisfy stringent minimum requirements, including the completion of at least 60 hours of coach-specific education, accumulating a minimum of 100 client-coaching experience hours (with at least 75 hours paid and 25 hours within the 18 months prior to application), and fulfilling 10 hours of mentor coaching over three months or longer.1 The ACC Exam serves as the final, critical step in this rigorous credentialing process, authorized only after staff review and verification confirm that all other qualifications—including education, experience, mentor coaching, and performance evaluation—have been successfully met.1


A.2. ACC Exam Blueprint: Domains, Weightings, and Format


The ACC Exam is administered as a computer-based assessment, delivered either at authorized Pearson VUE testing centers worldwide or through the Pearson OnVUE remote proctored testing service.1 The total examination time is scheduled for 90 minutes and is structured into two sections of 30 items each, separated by an optional scheduled break of 10 minutes.1
The examination consists of 60 total knowledge-based, multiple-choice items, each presenting four possible response options, of which only one is correct.1 The content is distributed across three broad domains, each representing core areas of professional practice required at the ACC level.1

A 14-day waiting period is required following the first attempt, and 30 days are required for subsequent attempts, with a maximum of six attempts permitted within a 12-month period.1


A.3. Logistical and Technical Requirements


Once applicants receive notification of approval, they are authorized to schedule the ACC Exam appointment with Pearson VUE. This appointment must be scheduled and completed within 60 days of receiving the authorization.1 Regardless of the delivery method chosen (in-center or remote), strict identification and security protocols are enforced.
Candidates must provide valid, government-issued photo identification (such as a passport or driver's license), ensuring the name used for registration exactly matches the name on the ID presented.1 A Candidate Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) must be accepted prior to beginning the exam, prohibiting the copying, sharing, or disclosure of test content to maintain integrity.1 Prohibited items in the testing area include mobile phones, smart devices, notes, and personal belongings, which must be secured in a locker (center testing) or removed from the remote testing space.1
For candidates utilizing the OnVUE remote proctoring service, a mandatory systems test must be completed beforehand to ensure the device and internet connection meet the necessary technical specifications (e.g., stable 6 Mbps down/3 Mbps up connection, specific OS versions, and a clear testing space).1 Remote proctors communicate solely in English, which may necessitate careful consideration of the testing environment.1
For international candidates or those testing in a non-English environment, language support is available. Candidates taking the exam with language aids (translation alongside English) automatically receive a 30-minute extension. Candidates whose primary language is not English and who reside in a non-English speaking country are also granted an automatic 30-minute time extension if language aids are unavailable in their language.1 Additional accommodations, such as extended time or access to auxiliary items for medical conditions, must be requested and approved by ICF Credentials and Standards prior to scheduling the exam appointment.1


A.4. The Criticality of the Professional Foundation (The 60% Rule)


A structural analysis of the ACC Exam blueprint reveals a crucial emphasis on professional governance. Domains 1 (Coaching Ethics) and 2 (Definition and Boundaries of Coaching) jointly constitute 60% of the examination content.1 This majority weighting confirms that the ICF’s core mandate for credentialing is centered on consumer protection and the ethical prevention of harm.1
The implication for candidate preparation is profound: while mastery of the Core Competencies (40%) is essential for demonstrating skill, proficiency in the two foundational domains is necessary to demonstrate fitness to practice safely. The examination rigorously tests the candidate's capability to navigate complex situations involving ethical breaches, conflicts of interest, and the necessity of referring clients to clinical professionals when their needs exceed the coach’s non-clinical scope.1 Therefore, successful completion of the ACC Exam hinges not merely on demonstrating effective coaching technique, but on the ability to operate within established legal and ethical guardrails, prioritizing professional responsibility and client safety above all else.


B. Part I: Domain 1 – Coaching Ethics (30%)


This domain requires detailed knowledge of the principles and standards governing the ethical conduct of all ICF professionals, with specific attention to the 2020 version of the ICF Code of Ethics.1


B.1. Foundational Ethical Practice (ICF Code of Ethics, 2020)


The ICF Code of Ethics describes the ICF core values, ethical principles, and specific ethical standards of behavior that must be applied consistently across all coaching-related interactions.1 The code serves multiple purposes: it sets rigorous standards of conduct, provides guidance for ethical reflection and decision-making, and forms the basis for ethics training.1 Adherence to the Code requires personal integrity, honesty, ongoing professional development, and a commitment to handling ethical dilemmas courageously.1
The Ethical Standards for ICF Professionals are structured into five key sections, which form the primary testing areas for this domain:
1. Agreements for Client and/or Sponsor Engagement: Communication regarding the nature of coaching, roles, responsibilities, confidentiality, and financial arrangements must occur before coaching commences.1
2. Confidentiality and Legal Compliance: Maintaining the highest level of privacy regarding client information and securely managing all records.1
3. Professional Conduct and Conflicts of Interest: Awareness, management, and resolution of any situation that might compromise the objectivity or integrity of the relationship.1
4. Commitment to Delivering Consistent Value: Continuous vigilance regarding the value received by the client and readiness to adjust the relationship or refer out if necessary.1
5. Professional Integrity and Accountability: Accurate representation of qualifications and adherence to the principle of "doing good" versus "avoiding bad".1


B.2. Confidentiality, Law, and Disclosure Protocols


Coaches are mandated to maintain the strictest level of confidentiality with all involved parties throughout the coaching engagement.1 This obligation extends beyond the verbal conversation to include the secure handling, storage, and disposal of all records and electronic files, ensuring compliance with applicable privacy laws and organizational policies.1
A critically tested concept in this domain is the knowledge of factors that necessitate breaching confidentiality, as confidentiality is not absolute.1 Candidates must understand the three primary exceptions that ethically and legally compel disclosure:
1. Imminent Risk of Danger: Disclosure is required when there is an imminent or likely risk of danger to the client (self-harm/suicide) or to other individuals.1 This exception prioritizes safety above privacy.
2. Legal Mandate: Confidentiality must be broken when disclosure is required by law, regulation, or a valid court order or subpoena.1 Compliance with relevant jurisdiction is paramount.
3. Explicit Agreement: Information may be exchanged among parties (client, sponsor, coach) only if a clear, specific agreement regarding what information will be shared and how it will be exchanged is established upfront in the coaching contract.1
The required depth of understanding here is procedural. The professional must first recognize the legal or safety trigger and then follow the steps for appropriate disclosure, ensuring ethical accountability is maintained.


B.3. Professional Conduct and Conflict Management


Knowledge of what constitutes a conflict of interest (COI) is explicitly required for the ACC Exam.1 A COI arises when an ICF professional is involved in multiple interests (e.g., financial, personal, or professional) where serving one interest could potentially conflict with or work against another, including perceived conflicts.1
The coach holds the responsibility for proactive COI management.1 This management involves:
1. Awareness and Disclosure: Being aware of and openly discussing the implications of any existing or potential multiple agreements or relationships with all involved parties.1
2. Resolution through Dialogue and Agreements: Managing the conflict through continuous self-reflection, modification of the coaching agreement, and ongoing transparent dialogue with the client and sponsor.1
3. Ultimate Resolution: If a conflict cannot be managed effectively, the coach must resolve the issue by suspending or ending the professional relationship.1
Furthermore, professional conduct dictates strict boundary management. Coaches must set clear, appropriate, and culturally sensitive boundaries governing all interactions.1 Specifically, engaging in any sexual or romantic relationship with a client or sponsor is strictly prohibited. If the coach detects any shift in the nature of the relationship, they must take immediate, appropriate action, including the cancellation of the coaching engagement.1


B.4. Ethical Priority and Preventative Practice


The rigorous testing of ethical conduct emphasizes the mandatory structure of ethical action within coaching practice. When faced with a dilemma, the coach must apply a hierarchy: immediate physical safety and legal requirements supersede the continuation of the coaching relationship or the principle of confidentiality.1 Scenarios will often test the candidate’s ability to correctly prioritize this sequence of actions: assessing threat or legal requirement, making necessary disclosures, and then addressing the viability of the relationship itself.
Furthermore, professional competence in ethics is fundamentally defined by preventative practice. The Code focuses heavily on establishing clear boundaries and agreements before potential conflicts arise.1 This proactive establishment of trust and professional boundaries is the highest form of ethical practice, ensuring that the coach maintains integrity and avoids situations that necessitate repair or remediation. Therefore, exam questions pertaining to conflicts of interest should be evaluated with a bias toward upfront, transparent contracting and immediate, decisive action when boundaries are compromised.


C. Part II: Domain 2 – Definition and Boundaries of Coaching (30%)


This domain ensures candidates understand the professional scope of coaching and their responsibility to differentiate it from related support modalities, protecting the client by knowing when to refer them to other professionals.1


C.1. Knowledge of the ICF Definition and Coaching Process


The International Coaching Federation (ICF) defines coaching as a partnership with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process designed to inspire them to maximize their personal and professional potential.1 This process is inherently client-driven and non-directive; the coach facilitates discovery, but the client defines the agenda and owns the resulting outcomes.5
The core philosophical distinction of coaching is its temporal focus: it is present- and future-focused, aiming to map the client's path forward (cartography) rather than excavate or heal past trauma (archaeology).4 Coaching is designed to work with individuals who are fundamentally functioning and seeking optimization, specific goal attainment, and enhanced accountability.6


C.2. Differentiation from Related Professions


The ACC candidate must demonstrate clear knowledge of how coaching differs from therapy, counseling, mentoring, and consulting.1 These distinctions are based on the focus of the intervention, the role of the professional, and the expected outcomes.

C.3. Recognizing Conditions Outside the Coaching Scope


A fundamental element of operating within professional boundaries is the ability to recognize when a client’s challenges are clinical in nature and impede their ability to engage productively in the coaching process.1 Coaching relies on the client's capacity for self-direction, resourcefulness, and accountability; conditions that undermine these capacities signal a need for clinical intervention.
Knowledge of the signs of mental health conditions that may block progress in coaching is crucial.1 These indicators fall into several categories 10:
* Emotional and Mood Indicators: Persistent or increasing feelings of sadness, despair, hopelessness, helplessness, or profound guilt. A significant decline in the ability to experience pleasure.
* Cognitive Indicators: Intrusive thoughts, severe inability to concentrate or focus, auditory or visual hallucinations, or fixation on past trauma.
* Physiological/Behavioral Indicators: Extreme changes in appetite (excessive increase or decrease), significant sleep disturbances (insomnia or excessive sleeping), being hyper-alert or excessively tired, or signs of substance abuse.
* High-Risk Indicators: Disclosure of current abuse, or any thought, plan, or attempt related to self-harm or harming others.11
If symptoms interfere with the client's ability to engage in goal-oriented, future-focused thinking, the coach's non-directive role is compromised, necessitating a referral.


C.4. When and How to Make Appropriate Referrals


When conditions outside the scope of coaching are recognized, the ACC candidate must know when and how to make an appropriate referral for mental health professionals.1 The coach's duty is to recommend services of other professionals when necessary.12
The referral process must be handled gently and collaboratively 8:
1. Recognition and Assessment: Identify that the client's needs exceed the scope of the coach’s competence.
2. Raise Concerns and Explain Boundaries: Gently bring the issues to the client's awareness. Crucially, the coach must explain that the symptoms or issues disclosed are outside the coach's training (scope limitation) and that clinical support is required for resolution.13 The coach must not attempt to diagnose or treat the clinical condition.6
3. Co-Creation of Referral: Partner with the client to research appropriate specialists, acting as a resource partner to identify providers who can help.8
4. Immediate Crisis Action: In cases of high-risk behavior (e.g., self-harm consideration), the coach must immediately take additional action, such as calling a suicide prevention hotline with the client present.11
5. Follow-Up: The coach may resume coaching once the client has established baseline mental health or if the clinical professional deems it complementary to treatment.6
The integrity of this domain relies on understanding that the coach works exclusively with the "functioning individual" seeking advancement, not stabilization.1 When cognitive or emotional challenges impede the client's capacity for creative self-management, the non-clinical coach must defer to licensed experts.


D. Part III: Domain 3 – Coaching Competencies, Strategies, and Techniques (40%)


As the largest single domain, this section tests the practical application of coaching skills and techniques, anchored entirely within the ICF Core Competencies framework.1


D.1. Knowledge of Client Contracting and Key Elements


Knowledge of how to contract with clients is a foundational skill in this domain 1, representing Competency 3: Establishes and Maintains Agreements. Effective contracting ensures procedural clarity and protects the relationship. This involves two levels of agreement: the overall engagement and the individual session.


D.1.1. Engagement-Level Contracting (The Overall Coaching Plan)


The coach must explain clearly what coaching is and is not, describing the process to the client and any relevant stakeholders.14 Key elements that must be agreed upon include 1:
* Roles and responsibilities of the client, coach, and sponsor.
* Logistics such as fees, scheduling, duration, and provisions for termination.
* Guidelines for confidentiality and how information will be shared.
* The establishment of the overall coaching plan and primary goals for the engagement.


D.1.2. Session-Level Contracting (The Session Goal)


Before each session begins, the coach must partner with the client to establish a clear focus 14:
* Identify or reconfirm the specific goal the client wishes to accomplish in that session.
* Define what the client believes must be addressed or resolved to achieve the session goal.
* Define or reconfirm the measures of success for the session's outcome.
* Partner with the client to manage the focus and time allotted for the conversation.


D.2. Mastery of ICF Core Competencies (2019/Latest)


The ICF Core Competencies (2019/latest) serve as the foundation for coaching practice and are grouped into four domains.15 The ACC exam tests the application of the behaviors defined within the ACC Minimum Skills Requirements (MSR), which provides the explicit performance standard for this credential level.1


D.2.1. Domain A: Foundation


1. Demonstrates Ethical Practice: This is tested primarily through Domain 1, but its application here emphasizes integrating the ICF Code of Ethics (2020) by maintaining confidentiality, acting with integrity, and appropriately distinguishing coaching from other modalities.14
2. Embodies a Coaching Mindset: The coach maintains an open, curious, and flexible mindset.14 This includes acknowledging the client's sole responsibility for their choices, engaging in reflective practice, remaining culturally aware, and utilizing self-awareness (intuition and emotional regulation) to remain present for the client.14


D.2.2. Domain B: Co-Creating the Relationship


3. Establishes and Maintains Agreements: Focuses on the contracting elements discussed in Section D.1. The coach partners to define logistics, overall goals, session goals, and measures of success, ensuring the coaching direction follows the client’s desired outcome.14
4. Cultivates Trust and Safety: Requires seeking to understand the client's context, identity, values, and beliefs. The coach demonstrates empathy and respect, adapting their approach to the client's style, and acknowledging the client's unique contributions to the process.14
5. Maintains Presence: The coach remains fully conscious and present, demonstrating focus, curiosity, and responsiveness. A key ACC skill here is being comfortable working in a space of "not knowing" and creating or allowing space for silence, pause, or reflection to allow the client to process and generate awareness.14


D.2.3. Domain C: Communicating Effectively


6. Listens Actively: This competency requires the coach to focus on both what is said and what is unsaid.14 The ACC coach must integrate the client's words, tone, and body language to fully understand the meaning. Key behaviors include reflecting or summarizing to ensure clarity, recognizing when more is being communicated (inquiring further), and noticing shifts in energy, emotions, or non-verbal cues.14
7. Evokes Awareness: The coach facilitates insight and learning. This is achieved through tools like powerful questioning, silence, and metaphor.14 The coach asks questions that challenge the client to explore beyond their current thinking, invites them to share their experience in the moment, and helps them identify factors influencing behavior patterns.14 The coach may also share observations or insights without attachment to create new client learning, and supports the client in reframing perspectives.14


D.2.4. Domain D: Cultivating Learning and Growth


8. Facilitates Client Growth: This final competency focuses on transforming insight into action and promoting client autonomy.14 The coach works with the client to integrate new awareness into their behaviors and partners with the client to design appropriate goals, actions, and accountability measures. Critically, the coach must acknowledge and support client autonomy in designing these measures, inviting them to consider necessary resources and potential barriers, and consistently celebrating their progress and successes.14


D.3. Knowledge of Goal Setting and Motivation


The ACC exam requires explicit knowledge of goal setting and motivation principles.1 Effective coaching relies on structured methods to ensure goals are actionable and aligned with client values.
Goal setting frameworks, such as the widely recognized GROW model (Goal, Reality, Options, Will/Way Forward) 17, provide a structured approach for guiding clients from defining their objective to committing to specific actions. Modern approaches, such as the DIVE IN Goals™ method, are also relevant, as they focus on maximizing the impact and alignment of goals beyond mechanical frameworks.18
Client motivation and accountability are fostered by designing clear action steps, empowering clients to take ownership of their development, and supporting client autonomy in choosing their methods of accountability.14 The coach acts as an unbiased thinking partner, holding clients accountable through regular sessions and follow-up, which is crucial for achieving sustainable personal and professional development.6


D.4. Knowledge of a Variety of Coaching Techniques, Tools, and Resources


The knowledge requirement includes familiarity with a variety of coaching techniques, tools, and resources used to facilitate the coaching process.1 These tools must support self-discovery and growth, aligning with the non-directive nature of coaching.19
Key techniques and tools include:
* Powerful, Open-ended Questioning: Artfully used (C7) to prompt deep reflection and help clients explore perspectives beyond their current thinking.14
* Active Listening and Synthesis: Techniques like reflecting and summarizing (C6) are tools used to ensure clarity and help the client synthesize information and discern patterns across the conversation.14
* Strategic Use of Silence and Pause: Employed (C5) to allow space for the client to reflect deeply, demonstrating the coach's comfortable presence in ambiguity.14
* Visualization and Metaphor: Techniques used to help clients explore options, mentally rehearse success, and overcome perceived obstacles, thereby staying motivated on their journey.17
* Sharing Observations Without Attachment: A technique (C7) where the coach offers insights and feelings observed during the session neutrally, facilitating new learning without steering the client's decision.14
The efficacy of coaching relies on knowing when and how to apply these evidence-based techniques, often rooted in positive psychology, to support a tactical action plan toward specific goals.6


D.5. The ACC Minimum Skills Requirements as the Standard


The assessment framework for this domain is directly derived from the ACC Minimum Skills Requirements (MSR).14 The MSR serves as the explicit operational definition of competence for the Associate Certified Coach level, providing clear guidelines on the minimum acceptable behaviors for each Core Competency.1
Because the ACC Exam is knowledge-based, its questions test the candidate's understanding of which course of action aligns with these published minimum standards, and which actions represent common coaching pitfalls identified by the ICF.14 For scenario-based questions, the correct response will be the one that is most client-centered, non-directive, and adheres to the specific behavioral indicators listed in the MSR (e.g., ensuring client autonomy in accountability design, or pausing the conversation to notice a non-verbal cue).14
A high degree of professionalism in this domain also necessitates the integration of contracting and growth. Effective application of Competency 8 (Facilitates Client Growth) is procedurally dependent on the clarity established in Competency 3 (Establishes Agreements).1 Failure to define goals, roles, or success metrics upfront inherently undermines the coach's ability to partner effectively in designing actionable outcomes, thereby ensuring that the foundational steps of the coaching process directly impact the possibility of client advancement.


E. Conclusion and Next Steps




E.1. Synthesizing Knowledge for Exam Application (Scenario Analysis)


The ACC Candidate Exam assesses the integration of knowledge across all three domains, primarily through scenario-based, knowledge-testing items.1 Preparation must focus on the critical synthesis of ethical mandates, professional boundaries, and core competency application.
1. Ethical Practice Scenarios (30%): Candidates must prioritize risk management. When presented with a potential breach, the response should first check for immediate harm or legal mandate (disclosure), followed by managing the resulting relationship consequences (contract amendment or termination).1
2. Boundary Scenarios (30%): The focus is on recognizing the scope of practice. If a client exhibits signs that compromise their capacity to function in a goal-oriented way (e.g., severe hopelessness, hallucinations, fixation on trauma), the correct response is referral, which must be executed collaboratively and non-diagnostically, maintaining the non-clinical boundary.1
3. Competency Scenarios (40%): Responses must align strictly with the ACC Minimum Skills Requirements.14 The best answer is always the most client-centered, non-directive action that demonstrates active listening (C6), powerful questioning (C7), and supports client autonomy and accountability (C8).14


E.2. Post-Exam Process and Credential Maintenance


Candidates receive preliminary results immediately upon completion of the computer-based exam. Official confirmation and score reports are provided shortly thereafter via the Pearson VUE profile.1 For those who are not successful, the score report offers targeted feedback on relative performance across the exam domains, which is intended to guide concentrated study and preparation for subsequent attempts.1


E.2.1. Credential Renewal Requirements


The ACC credential is valid for three years from the date of issue.1 To maintain the credential, holders must meet rigorous renewal requirements:
* Continuing Coach Education (CCE): A total of 40 hours of CCE must be completed within the three-year renewal period.1
* Core Competencies CCE: At least 24 hours of the CCE must be dedicated to ICF Core Competencies.1 This mandatory requirement ensures ongoing development of fundamental coaching skills.
* Coaching Ethics: Within the Core Competencies CCE, a minimum of three hours must be specifically dedicated to coaching ethics.1
* Mentor Coaching: Ten hours of Mentor Coaching are required over a period of three months or longer, including a minimum of three hours one-on-one with an eligible Mentor Coach.1
These renewal standards emphasize the ICF’s commitment to continuous professional and ethical development, ensuring that ACC holders maintain the required competence and uphold the standards of the profession beyond initial certification.1


E.3. Key Resources for Ongoing Study


The core preparation materials for achieving mastery in the ACC domains include:
* The ICF Code of Ethics (2020 version).
* The ICF Core Competencies (2019/latest model).
* The ACC Minimum Skills Requirements (MSR) document.
* The ICF official guides regarding differentiation of coaching modalities and procedures for referring a client to therapy.8

Domain 3 — Coaching Competencies, Strategies & Techniques (40%)
What the exam measures (official scope)
                                             * Contracting, ICF Core Competencies, goal setting & motivation, and a repertoire of coaching techniques/tools/resources.

Core areas to master
                                                * Coaching Agreement (contracting): Establish terms up front—roles, responsibilities, confidentiality, financials, cancellation, etc. This is a repeat high-yield theme across ethics & competencies.

                                                * Core Competencies update—emphases: ethical behavior & confidentiality; coaching mindset & reflective practice; distinctions between levels of agreement; partnership; cultural/systemic/contextual awareness. These are tested themes in scenarios.

                                                * Ethics integrated: Ethical practice is competency #1; expect situations weaving competence + ethics.

Technique & strategy patterns you’ll see in stems
                                                   * Partnering behaviors (co-creating agenda, measures of success) vs directive behaviors.

                                                   * Evoking awareness (questions, reflections, metaphors) → Client-generated actions with accountability & learning capture.

                                                   * Goal framing: Ensure goals are client-owned, meaning-linked, and measurable; tie back to motivation (values/identity).

                                                   * Cultural/systemic awareness: Acknowledge context and design experiments that fit the client’s environment.

Typical exam traps
                                                      * Coach over-structuring (coach designs the plan) vs co-design.

                                                      * Jumping to actions before fully establishing the agreement for the session and the desired outcome.

Decision mini-tree
                                                         1. Do we have a clear agreement/outcome for this session? If not → clarify first.

                                                         2. Who owns the agenda/action? The client should—favor evocation/partnership.

                                                         3. Did we capture learning & next steps? Close with insights + commitments + accountability.

Quick practice prompt:
The client asks you to “give three steps” they should take this week. Best response?
Answer: Explore desired outcomes and evoke the client’s options/criteria; co-design actions and accountability once the client generates possibilities. (Aligns with partnership & client agency.)

🩶 Domain 1 – Coaching Ethics (30%)
1.1 The ICF Code of Ethics (2020)
Purpose. The Code establishes trust in the coaching profession by ensuring consistency, integrity, and client protection.
Structure. Five sections:
                                                                  1. Responsibility to Clients — clarity of agreements, confidentiality, informed consent.

                                                                  2. Responsibility to Practice and Performance — continual learning, supervision, reflection.

                                                                  3. Responsibility to Professionalism — honesty, transparency, avoiding misrepresentation.

                                                                  4. Responsibility to Society — respect, inclusion, contribution beyond the coaching room.

                                                                  5. Responsibility to ICF — compliance, cooperation with ethics review.

In practice. Before every engagement, confirm the client understands coaching, its limits, and confidentiality boundaries. Review these periodically.
Maintain reflective notes—not judgmental assessments—and store them securely.
Exam focus. Scenarios often test whether you apply ethics before coaching begins (through agreements). The correct answer is rarely “continue and discuss later.” Pause and clarify first.
________________


1.2 Conflicts of Interest
Definition. Any situation in which personal, professional, or financial interests could influence objectivity.
Examples. Coaching a direct report, close friend, or family member; accepting gifts; promoting a product.
Best practice. Disclose all potential conflicts, obtain written consent, and, if bias cannot be managed, refer the client elsewhere.
Exam cue. If a coach has dual roles (manager + coach), the best option is to → refer or re-contract with full transparency.
________________


1.3 Confidentiality and Legal Compliance
Principle. Information from coaching remains private unless:
                                                                     * law or court order requires disclosure,

                                                                     * there is imminent danger to self or others, or

                                                                     * the client consents in writing.
Maintain compliant record storage and clarify digital data use.

Exam cue. Keywords like harm, mandatory reporting, subpoena → correct action is to notify client and disclose appropriately, not silence or gossip.
________________


1.4 Technology & AI Ethics
Reality. You remain responsible for confidentiality even when using apps, AI note-takers, or cloud storage.
Obtain explicit client consent, anonymize data, and verify system security.
Exam cue. If an AI transcription service is mentioned, safest answer → seek consent and ensure data protection.
________________


1.5 Professional Conduct and Accountability
Competent coaches:
                                                                        * engage in ongoing education,

                                                                        * participate in supervision/mentor coaching,

                                                                        * own mistakes transparently,

                                                                        * report unethical conduct through proper channels.

Exam cue. When an error occurs, correct → inform the client and ICF if necessary; wrong → “ignore and move on.”

💙 Domain 2 – Definition and Boundaries of Coaching (30%)
2.1 Definition of Coaching
“Partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential.”
Core ideas. Partnership = equality; thought-provoking = evoking awareness; creative process = tailored exploration.
Coaching is forward-looking and capability-building, not remedial.
Exam cue. Options with verbs tell, advise, train → not coaching.
Options with ask, explore, co-create → correct.
________________


2.2 Coaching vs Other Disciplines
Discipline
	Focus
	Coach vs Other
	Therapy
	Healing past trauma
	Coach avoids diagnosing; may refer
	Consulting
	Solving business problems
	Coach facilitates client discovery
	Mentoring
	Passing experience
	Coach draws insight from client
	Training
	Teaching skills
	Coach explores application, not content delivery
	Exam cue. Look for language showing the coach acting as an expert → incorrect. The exam rewards facilitative stance.
________________


2.3 Referral and Mental-Health Awareness
Indicators for referral. Emotional instability, suicidal ideation, traumatic flashbacks, or expectation of therapy.
Steps. Pause coaching → express concern → refer to licensed professional → document and follow up appropriately.
Never attempt diagnosis.
Exam cue. The safest answer is to refer or re-contract, not “coach through it.”
________________


2.4 Contracting and Boundaries
Two levels.
                                                                           1. Engagement Contract: duration, fees, confidentiality, goals, responsibilities.

                                                                           2. Session Contract: “What do you want to take away today?”

Set limits on time, contact, and dual relationships. Revisit as context changes.
Exam cue. When a session lacks clarity, first step → establish agreement.
________________


💜 Domain 3 – Coaching Competencies, Strategies & Techniques (40%)
3.1 Core Competencies Clusters
                                                                              1. Foundation – Ethical Practice & Mindset

                                                                              2. Co-Creating the Relationship – Agreements, Trust, Presence

                                                                              3. Communicating Effectively – Listening & Evoking Awareness

                                                                              4. Cultivating Learning and Growth – Facilitating Client Growth

Each competency represents observable behaviors assessed in both performance evaluation and exam scenarios.
Exam cue. Match key verbs:
                                                                                 * “Explores,” “co-creates,” “notices,” “evokes” = competency language.

                                                                                 * “Tells,” “advises,” “fixes” = not coaching.

________________


3.2 Goal Setting and Motivation
Principles. Goals emerge from the client’s values and desired outcomes.
Use SMART or meaning-based framing; revisit progress regularly.
Link motivation to autonomy, mastery, and purpose.
Exam cue. Coach-driven goals → wrong; client-generated goals → right.
________________


3.3 Behavior Change and Learning
Use reflection, accountability, and experimentation rather than prescription.
Encourage awareness of limiting beliefs and reframe toward strengths.
Exam cue. Answers where the coach “assigns homework” without exploration miss the partnership principle.
________________


3.4 Tools and Models
                                                                                    * GROW Model (G oal → R eality → O ptions → W ill)

                                                                                    * Wheel of Life for balance assessment

                                                                                    * Values clarification, future-self visualization, metaphors

Use tools only with permission and flexibility.
Exam cue. The correct choice demonstrates adapting the tool to the client, not imposing the tool.
________________


3.5 Cultural and Contextual Awareness
Recognize systemic factors, culture, identity, and privilege.
Adopt curiosity over assumption; adjust communication style.
Acknowledge and explore cultural meaning when relevant.
Exam cue. Answers emphasizing curiosity and respect for client worldview are correct; imposing “universal truth” is not.
________________


3.6 Evaluating Progress and Closure
Review. “What insights did you gain? How will you apply them?”
Document outcomes, celebrate achievements, and co-design next steps or closure.
Exam cue. Final step should be reflect + celebrate + acknowledge learning, not “simply end the contract.”
________________


🧠 Exam-Wide Strategy Summary
                                                                                       * Read verbs: partner, evoke, explore = likely correct.

                                                                                       * Protect client autonomy above all.

                                                                                       * Clarify before acting.

                                                                                       * Break confidentiality only for law or safety.

                                                                                       * Choose presence > performance.

Domain 1 Sample Scenarios
                                                                                          1. Confidentiality Breach
 A client reveals intent to harm a coworker. What’s your next step?
A Keep it confidential B Explore emotions C Follow legal/ethical disclosure D Call HR anonymously
Answer: C – Disclosure per law + notify client.
Competency: Demonstrates Ethical Practice.

                                                                                          2. Conflict of Interest
 You’re asked to coach your own employee.
Answer: Disclose and refer to an independent coach.

Domain 2 Sample Scenarios
                                                                                             1. Therapeutic Boundary
 A client discusses trauma and asks for advice.
Answer: Acknowledge topic, clarify coaching scope, and refer to therapist.
Trap: “Continue to coach the feelings.”

                                                                                             2. Contract Clarity
 Client says, “Let’s just talk.”
Answer: Ask what would make the conversation most useful today → establish session agreement.

Domain 3 Sample Scenarios
                                                                                                1. Goal Ownership
 Coach defines next steps for the client.
Correct answer: Re-engage client to generate their own actions.

                                                                                                2. Cultural Sensitivity
 Client expresses a cultural belief you don’t share.
Correct answer: Acknowledge perspective, stay curious, invite exploration of its meaning.

                                                                                                3. Session Closure
 Client finishes action plan quickly. What next?
Correct answer: Invite reflection on learning and next commitments.
